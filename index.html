What I've built

Semester: a deadline planner for my courses

Four courses, two inboxes, and a degree audit, pulled into one note that tells me what's due and what to do first.

What it does
Every 30 minutes it reads Canvas, Outlook, Gmail, and my degree audit and sorts what's due. It drafts replies for me to look at. If the model can't be reached, a rule-based plan takes over.
The decision that mattered most
It drafts. It never sends, submits, or enrolls. The rule it reads on every run:
Never send email, submit work, enroll, or change accounts without my explicit approval. Draft, then wait.
My overrides always win.
What went wrong
In 47 days the model call failed 804 times; the most common recorded cause was an expired login. The live Canvas connection was unavailable at some point on 36 of those days. Twice it stopped tracking an overdue item it couldn't verify instead of asking me. The rule-based fallback kept the plan running through all of it.

Sources: semester/out/run.log and decisions.log (Sep 2 – Oct 18, 2026) · semester/config/instructions.md · semester/README.md

Council: can many cheap models match one strong one?

A test bench for one question: can a group of very cheap models stand in for a strong one, and where do they fall short?

What I measured
220 samples across 18 problems, plus head-to-head trials on an open research problem.
What went wrong
Four bugs in my own grader each made the models look more capable than they were. Every headline number I had before the fix was wrong. I rebuilt the grader with 18 unit tests. Separately, once I validated the AI judge, 17 of 42 earlier verdicts flipped.
The decision that mattered most
Keep every overturned claim on the record. If a results log can delete its own mistakes, nobody can trust it, including me.
What it found
On problems with one checkable answer, the group added nothing over a simple vote. On an open problem, the cheap group found a flaw the strong model missed, at about 1/70 of the cost. The strong model was still better at building the solution. That was one trial, and I was the judge, so I hold it loosely.

Source: council/FINDINGS.md (August 2026)

Blinx Co-op: a 2002 Xbox game, online for my friends and family

Mac, Windows, and browser versions of Blinx: The Time Sweeper, with up to six players online. Built with Claude: first file decoded October 3, 2026; Mac and Windows releases for friends by October 10.

What it took
Decoding the game's file formats, rebuilding it to run natively, and adding online play that keeps every player's game identical. The releases update themselves.
The decision that mattered most
Ship no game files. Each player uses their own copy of the game, and everything else gets built from it on their machine.
What went wrong
Keeping the Mac and Windows builds identical took two fixes: matching the original console's number rounding exactly, and finding two errors in the decompiled code. I checked 14 stages: 0 desyncs.

Sources: blinx/STATUS.md · blinx/JOURNAL.md (October 2026). The release itself is private.

How I check AI's work

  • Every claim ends in a fact, a definition, or "unknown." I wrote a procedure that keeps asking "why is that true, and how do we know?" until each step bottoms out. I revised it on September 10, 2026, after I caught it accepting a sourced fact inside an invalid inference.
  • Facts get checked at their source. A fact-checking agent I set up reads the actual papers on PubMed and the primary sources, then reports a verdict with its confidence.
  • No building on unconfirmed facts. If something can't be confirmed in its primary source, the work stops until it can.
  • Test the tester. From Council: "An unverified harness manufactures capability that isn't there."
